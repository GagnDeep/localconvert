How to compress a video on iPhone without losing quality

Video too big to send? Learn how bitrate, resolution and codec affect file size, and how to shrink iPhone videos by 50–90% while keeping them sharp.

A one-minute 4K video from a recent iPhone can easily top 300 MB. That's too big for most email providers, slow to upload on mobile data, and often rejected by messaging apps that cap or crush large files. The good news: most of that size is unnecessary for the way you'll actually watch the video.

This guide explains what makes video files big, how to shrink them intelligently, and the settings that give the biggest savings with the least visible difference.

Why iPhone videos are so large

File size is almost entirely determined by bitrate — how many bits per second the encoder spends describing the picture — multiplied by duration. Your iPhone records at a high bitrate because it's capturing a master copy: detail you might want to crop, colour-grade or watch on a big TV.

Three settings drive that bitrate:

  • Resolution. 4K has four times as many pixels as 1080p.
  • Frame rate. 60 fps needs noticeably more data than 30 fps.
  • Codec. HEVC (H.265) is roughly 40–50% more efficient than H.264 at the same quality.

The settings that matter most

1. Resolution: match it to the screen

If the video will be watched on a phone, 1080p is already sharper than most people can perceive at arm's length. Dropping from 4K to 1080p alone typically cuts size by 60–75%. For quick clips in chats, 720p is often perfectly fine.

2. Codec: HEVC when you can, H.264 when you must

HEVC gives the same quality at around half the size of H.264 and is supported on every Apple device and most modern phones and browsers. Choose H.264 only if the recipient has an old computer or the destination (some upload portals, older smart TVs) specifically asks for it.

3. Bitrate: let a target size decide

Instead of guessing numbers, tell your compressor how big the file needs to be. LocalConvert's Compress Video tool has presets like Fits in 25 MB: it calculates the bitrate from the duration so the result lands just under the limit with the best quality possible.

4. Trim before you compress

The cheapest bytes are the ones you don't keep. Cutting the shaky first five seconds and the awkward ending often saves more than any encoder setting. Use Trim Video first — when trimming alone, LocalConvert can pass through the original video without re-encoding, so quality is untouched.

Built-in options on iPhone

iOS has a few native ways to send big videos without compressing:

  • Mail Drop in the Mail app uploads attachments up to 5 GB to iCloud and emails a link instead.
  • iCloud links from Photos (Share › Copy iCloud Link) let others download the full-quality original.
  • Recording settings in Settings › Camera › Record Video let you capture at 1080p 30 fps by default if you rarely need 4K.

These avoid quality loss but require the recipient to download a large file, and links expire. When you need an attachment that just works, compression is the practical answer.

What "without losing quality" really means

Every lossy re-encode discards some information — the question is whether you can see it. At sensible settings (1080p HEVC for phone viewing), most people cannot distinguish the compressed clip from the original side by side. Problems appear when you push too far: blocky dark scenes, smeared fine textures like grass or water, and banding in skies. If you notice those, step up one quality level.

Also avoid compressing the same video repeatedly. Keep the original and always compress from it.

Should you make a GIF instead?

For short reaction clips with no sound, a GIF autoplays everywhere — but GIFs are surprisingly inefficient. A three-second GIF can be larger than a 30-second MP4. If the destination supports video, prefer MP4. If you do need a GIF, Video to GIF lets you keep it small by lowering frame rate and width.

Common questions

Does compressing remove HDR?

iPhone videos are often recorded in Dolby Vision HDR. When you compress with HEVC at the original resolution, LocalConvert keeps the HDR signal where the output supports it. Converting to H.264 produces a standard dynamic range video, which looks correct on every screen but loses the extra brightness range on HDR displays.

Why does my compressed video look worse in dark scenes?

Dark, low-contrast areas are where encoders save bits most aggressively, so blockiness appears there first. Step up from Smallest to Balanced, or from 720p to 1080p, and the problem usually disappears.

Can I compress several videos at once?

Yes. Select multiple clips and LocalConvert processes them one after another, showing progress for each, so you can put your phone down and come back to a finished batch.

Is audio compressed too?

Audio is a small fraction of a video's size, so LocalConvert keeps it at a high-quality AAC bitrate. If you don't need the sound at all, Mute Video removes it entirely.

Summary

  • Trim first — it's free quality.
  • Drop to 1080p for anything watched on phones.
  • Use HEVC unless compatibility demands H.264.
  • Use a target-size preset for email and chat limits.
  • Compress on-device so private videos never leave your phone.
