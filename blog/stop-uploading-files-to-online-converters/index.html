Why you should stop uploading files to online converters

Online converters get a copy of every passport, contract and family photo you upload. What happens to your files — and the safer, on-device alternative.

By the LocalConvert Team

Search "convert PDF to JPG" and the first page is full of free websites that do it in seconds. They're convenient, and most are run by legitimate companies. But every one of them works the same way: you upload your file to a computer you don't control, it's processed there, and you download the result. Think about what you've converted this year — tax forms, a passport scan, a signed lease, photos of your kids — and where those copies might be now.

What happens when you upload a file

  1. Your file travels over the internet to the service's servers, often operated by a cloud provider in another country.
  2. It's written to disk so it can be processed. Your converted file is written to disk too.
  3. Both are kept for some period — commonly anywhere from one hour to several days — so you can download the result.
  4. They may appear in backups, logs and caches that live longer than the stated retention window.

Reputable services encrypt transfers and delete files on a schedule. But you're relying on promises in a privacy policy, the security of their infrastructure, and the honesty of everyone with access to it.

The real risks

Data breaches

Any service that stores files is a target. A breach exposes whatever was on the servers at that moment — which, for a popular converter, is a large pile of personal documents uploaded by strangers who never expected to be in the news.

Unclear retention and reuse

Terms of service vary widely. Some reserve broad rights to process uploaded content, use it to "improve services", or share it with partners. Few people read these before dragging in a PDF.

Malicious look-alikes

Security researchers and law-enforcement agencies have warned about fake converter sites that deliver malware in the "converted" download, or harvest the documents people upload. They look identical to legitimate tools and often rank well in search ads.

Metadata you didn't mean to share

Photos carry GPS location. Office documents carry author names and edit history. Uploading the file hands over everything inside it, not just what you see. See how to remove location from photos.

Upload limits, ads and waiting

Privacy aside, online converters have practical downsides: size caps on free plans, queues at busy times, pop-ups, upsells to subscriptions, and — on a phone — slow uploads over mobile data for large videos. A five-minute 4K clip can be over a gigabyte; uploading it just to convert it to MP4 is wasteful.

The alternative: convert on your own device

Modern phones are astonishingly capable. The chip in a current iPhone includes dedicated hardware for decoding and encoding HEVC and H.264 video, image processing pipelines for RAW photos, and a fast neural engine. Apple's frameworks — the same ones that power Photos, Files and Preview — can read and write a huge range of formats.

  • Nothing to breach. Your files never reach a server, so they can't leak from one.
  • No limits. The only cap is your free storage.
  • Faster for big files. No upload or download — hardware encoders often beat real time.
  • Works offline. On a plane, abroad, or with mobile data off.

When online tools are fine

Converting a public image you found on the web, or a meme, carries little risk. The question to ask is simple: *would I be comfortable if this file appeared somewhere public?* If not, convert it locally.

On a computer and need to convert an image right now? Our browser image converter runs entirely inside your browser — there's no upload step at all.

How to spot a risky converter site

  • It's reached through a search ad rather than an organic result, and the domain looks slightly off.
  • The "converted" download is an .exe, .dmg, .zip or a browser extension instead of the file type you asked for.
  • It asks you to install software or allow notifications before converting.
  • The privacy policy is missing, vague, or doesn't say when files are deleted.
  • There's no company name, address or contact information.

If any of these apply, close the tab. A conversion is never worth installing unknown software for.

"But the site says files are deleted after an hour"

Deletion promises are worth something, but they only cover what the company controls and intends. They can't account for a server that's already been compromised, a misconfigured storage bucket, an employee with too much access, or a law-enforcement request in another jurisdiction. On-device processing removes the question entirely: there is no copy anywhere else to delete.

What about documents at work?

Many organisations prohibit uploading company documents to third-party services because it can breach confidentiality agreements and data-protection rules such as GDPR. Converting on a managed device keeps the data within your organisation's control. If you handle client files, patient information or anything under NDA, on-device conversion is the conservative and compliant choice.

A simple rule

Documents with names, numbers, signatures or faces stay on your device. Use on-device tools for PDF compression, making PDFs from photos, and converting HEIC to JPG or Word to PDF. It's faster, it's free of limits, and it's the only approach where privacy is guaranteed by design rather than by policy.
