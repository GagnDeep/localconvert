HEIC vs JPG: which photo format should you use on iPhone?

HEIC photos are half the size of JPGs, but not everything can open them. Here's when to keep HEIC, when to switch to JPG, and how to convert in seconds.

If you've ever emailed a photo from your iPhone and been told it "won't open", you've met HEIC. Since iOS 11, iPhones have saved photos in the High Efficiency Image Container format instead of the decades-old JPG. It's a genuinely better format — but "better" doesn't help when an upload form, an older Windows laptop or a print kiosk refuses your file.

This guide explains what actually differs between HEIC and JPG, which one you should shoot in, and the fastest ways to convert between them without losing quality or privacy.

The short answer

Keep shooting in HEIC, and convert to JPG only when you need to share with something that can't handle it. HEIC saves roughly half the storage for the same visual quality, supports HDR and depth data, and every Apple device opens it natively. JPG is the universal fallback: it opens on every device, website and app made in the last thirty years.

What is HEIC?

HEIC is an image stored in the HEIF container and compressed with HEVC (H.265) — the same codec family used for 4K video. Because HEVC is far more modern than the JPEG algorithm from 1992, it can describe the same image with much less data. Apple adopted it in 2017, and it has been the default on every iPhone since.

HEIC also does things JPG simply can't:

  • 10-bit colour and HDR. Bright skies and deep shadows keep smooth gradients instead of banding.
  • Transparency and depth maps. Portrait mode stores depth information so you can change the blur later.
  • Multiple images in one file. Bursts and Live Photo stills can share a container.
  • Smaller files. Typically 40–50% smaller than an equivalent JPG.

What is JPG?

JPG (or JPEG) is the format that made digital photography practical. It compresses photos by discarding fine detail the eye is unlikely to notice, and its biggest strength today is compatibility: browsers, email clients, social networks, printers, TVs, cameras and every operating system can open a JPG without thinking.

Its weaknesses are age-related. JPG is limited to 8-bit colour, has no transparency, and shows blocky artefacts if you compress too hard or re-save repeatedly.

When you should convert HEIC to JPG

  • Uploading to government portals, job applications, school systems or online shops that only list "JPG, PNG".
  • Sending photos to someone on an older Windows PC or Android phone.
  • Printing at kiosks or ordering prints online.
  • Editing in older software that predates HEIF support.
  • Attaching photos to documents, presentations or websites.

You don't need to convert when sharing between Apple devices, uploading to iCloud, or posting through apps that convert automatically (most social apps do).

Option 2: Change how iPhone exports photos

Go to Settings › Apps › Photos and under Transfer to Mac or PC choose Automatic. When you import photos to a computer over a cable, iOS converts them to JPG on the fly. This doesn't help with email, AirDrop or uploads, but it's handy for Windows backups.

Option 3: Shoot in JPG

In Settings › Camera › Formats, choose Most Compatible. Your iPhone will save JPGs from now on. We don't recommend this: you'll use roughly twice the storage and lose HDR detail, just to avoid an occasional conversion.

On a Mac, you can also open a HEIC in Preview and choose File › Export to save as JPG. For dozens of photos, batch converting on your iPhone is much faster.

Does converting HEIC to JPG lose quality?

Technically, yes — converting from one lossy format to another always re-encodes the image. In practice, a high-quality JPG conversion (around 85–90%) is indistinguishable from the original at normal viewing sizes. What you lose is the extended HDR range and depth data, which JPG can't store.

If you need a lossless copy for editing, convert HEIC to PNG or HEIC to TIFF instead. Files will be much larger, but nothing is discarded.

A note on privacy

Every iPhone photo carries metadata: the date, the camera settings, and — unless you've turned it off — the exact GPS location where it was taken. When you convert a photo, the metadata usually travels with it. If you're posting publicly or sending to someone you don't know well, use Remove Location & Metadata first. The bottom line

HEIC is the better format for storing photos; JPG is the better format for sharing them with the rest of the world. Leave your camera on High Efficiency, and convert to JPG at the moment you share — ideally on your own device, in a couple of taps.
